Motivations

I built this as my first Python-driven GIS project. I have a B.S. in Chemistry and an M.S. in Geography, and soil chemistry draws from both disciplines. I've always been interested in how human activity, farming, livestock, land use, produce effects on the environment and downstream on human health and economic output. This project isn't about pollution directly, but it establishes a baseline before layering anything else on top of it. The next projects in this series build from here toward questions about livestock density, groundwater nitrate, and crop yield.

Nebraska was a natural starting point having recently moved here, and it turned out to be an interesting case. The state spans a major environmental transition from the Great Plains in the east to the high plains and foothills in the west, which drives variation in soil chemistry across a single state worth analyzing.

Overview

This project maps county-level soil pH and organic matter across Nebraska, then examines whether and how the two variables relate to each other. Both are fundamental indicators of soil health. pH affects nutrient availability and microbial activity, while organic matter drives fertility, water retention, and carbon storage. Understanding their distribution and relationship across the state is a first step toward more targeted questions about land use, agricultural productivity, and environmental health.

Methods

Soil chemistry data was queried from the USDA SSURGO database via the Soil Data Access REST API, a SQL-queryable interface that returns soil property measurements for every mapped soil component in Nebraska. pH and organic matter values were aggregated to the county level using component-weighted averages, where each soil component's contribution is weighted by its proportional coverage within the map unit. County boundaries came from the Census TIGER/Line dataset, loaded directly via URL to keep the notebook fully reproducible without local shapefiles.

USDA Major Land Resource Area (MLRA) boundaries were fetched from the NRCS ArcGIS Feature Service and clipped to Nebraska, covering 16 MLRAs. MLRAs are federally defined regions of similar soils, climate, water resources, and land use, making them a more meaningful grouping unit for soil chemistry than county boundaries alone. Nebraska falls within four Land Resource Regions: the Lake States and Central Great Plains (F), the Central Feed Grains and Livestock (G), the Central Great Plains Winter Wheat and Range (H), and the Central Feed Grains and Livestock region extending into the Corn Belt (M). Each county was assigned its dominant MLRA by area-weighted spatial overlay.

Physical soil sampling site locations came from the NCSS Lab Data Mart, a separate database from SSURGO that records actual sampling points collected in the field by USDA scientists. These were included to show where direct measurements exist versus where SSURGO values are interpolated from soil series models. Seven Nebraska counties had zero sampling sites on record.

Statistical analysis included Pearson correlation, linear regression, Moran's I spatial autocorrelation using Queen contiguity weights, one-way ANOVA, and Tukey's HSD post-hoc test for pairwise MLRA comparisons.

Findings

The clearest result was the east-west pH gradient, alkaline soils concentrated in the western Panhandle and more neutral to mildly acidic conditions in the east. Moran's I for pH came back at 0.78 (p = 0.001), confirming the pattern is spatially coherent rather than random noise. The mechanism is well established: higher precipitation in eastern Nebraska leaches carbonates from the soil profile over time, lowering pH. ANOVA confirmed significant pH differences across all 16 MLRAs (F = 15.8, p < 0.001), with 26 significant pairwise differences in the Tukey HSD results.

The organic matter results were less expected. Counties along Nebraska's major rivers, the Missouri and the Platte, were expected to show elevated organic matter due to alluvial deposition and high agricultural productivity. Instead, the highest values were concentrated in the Sandhills, a region of central Nebraska characterized by native grass prairie and minimal tillage. Arthur County averaged around 8.6% OM, well above the range for agricultural counties elsewhere in the state. The undisturbed prairie soil explains the accumulation. All six statistically significant Tukey HSD pairs for organic matter involved Sandhills MLRAs. Outside that region, OM differences between land resource areas were largely not significant.

Weak pH-OM correlation
Pearson r = -0.20 (p = 0.056). Borderline significant and practically weak. pH explains only about 4% of OM variance at the county level. The relationship exists in the expected direction, but other factors are driving most of the variation.

Strong spatial clustering in both variables
Moran's I: pH = 0.78, OM = 0.55 (both p = 0.001). Neighboring counties tend to have similar values, meaning the data contains less truly independent information than 92 observations would suggest. Standard significance tests do not account for this and should be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

Several methodological constraints limit how far the findings can be extended and point toward areas requiring further study.

Spatial stats
The Pearson correlation and regression p-values (both 0.056) should be interpreted carefully. A Moran's I of 0.78 for pH means neighboring counties are highly similar, and the data has far less independent information than 92 observations would imply. The borderline significance may be a product of spatial autocorrelation rather than a real signal.
Scale
Aggregating to the county level smooths over substantial within-county variation. Nebraska counties are large, and soil chemistry can shift considerably over short distances. A finer spatial unit, such as map unit or watershed, would likely reveal patterns that county averages obscure.
Other drivers
pH accounts for roughly 4% of OM variance. The remaining variance is likely attributable to land use, vegetation cover, soil parent material, precipitation, and temperature, among other factors. Soil parent material is a plausible candidate given Nebraska's east-west environmental transition, but isolating individual drivers would require additional variables and analysis beyond the scope of this project.
Data sources
SSURGO values are modeled from soil series correlations rather than direct field measurements for every polygon. Seven counties had zero NCSS pedon sampling sites on record, meaning their SSURGO-derived values are entirely model-based. The NCSS pedon dataset and SSURGO are also independent sources and cannot be used to validate one another directly.
